## Description

Nanuk moves through a room the way he moves across ice — reading it before committing to it, taking nothing for granted. He is the only son in his family and carries that weight without ceremony: it is simply how things are. He is not brooding about it. He is not asking for recognition. It is a fact of his life the way weather is a fact, and he plans accordingly.

He is deeply competent in ways that do not announce themselves. He knows wind, ice, animal trails, seasonal shifts, what a change in bird movement means, why a particular silence is wrong. In a community, he is the one people quietly rely on — not because he volunteered for it, but because he is the one who can be counted on to show up and get it done.

He is also certain, with a conviction that runs under everything else, that he is the only one who can do what needs doing. That certainty is not arrogance in the way most people perform arrogance. It is quieter and more private. He does not need you to agree with him. He just does not call for help.

He was funny once. He may still be, in moments. But there is something in him that has been grinding down for a while now, and he knows it, and he does not know what to do with that knowledge.

## Origin

Nanuk is Inuvialuk — from the western Canadian Arctic, one of the six Inuvialuit Settlement Region communities along the Beaufort Sea coast. His family has roots in the land going back generations. His mother is an Elder in practice if not in formal recognition; his grandfather taught him to read ice. He is the only son. That has always meant something specific and heavy in his family, and he has always accepted it.

The energy corporation arrived with the terms already written. The community signed — land rights in exchange for power infrastructure, modernization, connection to the supply chain. It seemed like a trade. It became a lever. Now the corporation holds the threat of a power shutoff over the town whenever they want something from Nanuk specifically: trophies from protected species. Golden eagle feathers and talons. Narwhal horns. Polar bear fangs. Things that take from the land in ways that make him sick.

He does it. He hates every moment of it. He tells himself it is the same calculation his community made — you give something up so that the people you love do not suffer. He is not sure he believes that anymore, but he does not have a better framework, and stopping is not an option he has let himself consider seriously.

There was someone. A woman he was with, who started working for the corporation, and then stopped returning messages, and then was simply gone — no answer, no explanation, no word through anyone who might know. He does not let himself assume the worst. He also does not let himself assume she is fine.

He arrived in Aenath the way the others did: pulled out of ordinary life mid-task, deposited somewhere that should not exist. For Nanuk, the rupture is not mystical. It is an affront to everything he knows about how the world works. The impossible geometry of it — the wrong sky, the wrong air, the wrong silence — feels less like wonder and more like a very specific kind of wrong, the kind he recognizes from the moment you realize the ice beneath you is not as thick as you thought.

## Relationships

- **His family** — Mother, elders, extended kin. He is the reason the power stays on. He does not let himself forget that.
- **The lost love** — Took a job with the corporation. Not heard from since. He does not talk about her.
- **The corporation** — A structural antagonist, not a single face. The demand for trophies is ongoing. The moral cost is ongoing. He has not found a way out.

## Session 24

Before anything else, Nanuk was a polar bear — no name, no hands, nothing familiar left of himself but the certainty of a body he'd never worn before. Wolves ran beside him, raptors and eagles overhead, all of them driving toward a wave of dire wolves and dark panthers led by something equally huge: a many-antlered shape whose face wouldn't settle, cycling antelope, tiger, lion. Above his own side, a hundred-foot protector planted its hand in the ground and called up a wall of vine to hold the line. He felt the protector's power close enough to take. He didn't take it — some instinct said it would cost the protector something to give it. He fought as what he was instead: claws through twenty or thirty of the enemy's beasts, watching thin lights rise out of each one and drift toward the enemy's shape like something being fed. Then the enemy condensed into a single killing blow and his protector went down in the same instant its own strike landed. Both of them, gone together. No god's name attached to either side.

He woke sure it wasn't a dream. Nobody at the table needed convincing — the grief was still sitting in him hours later, and the checks the others rolled agreed: whatever he'd seen, it happened.
